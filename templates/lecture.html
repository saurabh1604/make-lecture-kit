Reading a Straight Line

Every straight line is a promise: start somewhere, then change by the same amount for every step you take. That promise has a name — a linear function — and once you can read one, you can read almost any simple model.

Analogy first. A taxi fare works exactly like a line. You pay a flat fee just for getting in (the starting point), then a fixed amount for every mile after that (the steepness). Ride zero miles and you still owe the flat fee; ride ten miles and you owe the fee plus ten times the per-mile rate.

Step 1 — the two knobs

A straight line is controlled by exactly two numbers. The slope \(m\) is how much \(y\) changes for every one-unit increase in \(x\) — the per-mile rate. The intercept \(b\) is the value of \(y\) when \(x=0\) — the flat fee.

$$ y \;=\; mx + b $$
Worked example, real numbers. A taxi charges a flat \(b=\$3\) fee plus \(m=\$2\) per mile.
For a 5-mile ride: \(y = 2(5) + 3 = 10 + 3 = 12\). The fare is \$12.
For a 0-mile ride: \(y = 2(0) + 3 = 3\) — just the flat fee, exactly as \(b\) promised.

⚡ Drag the line, read the numbers

Slide \(m\) and \(b\) and watch the line redraw. The readout below recomputes \(y\) at the marked point every time you move a slider — the picture and the arithmetic always agree.

Where this shows up in ML

Linear regression is exactly this idea with more inputs: \(y = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + b\). Learning a model often means learning the best \(m\)'s and \(b\) from data — the same two knobs, just many more of them.

Pitfall — a line only fits what it can fit. Real data is rarely perfectly straight, and a line fit to a narrow range of \(x\) can be badly wrong far outside that range. Never trust a straight line's extrapolation past the data it was drawn from.
